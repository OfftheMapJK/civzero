# Recycle urine as fertiliser

## 1. The idea

Each person produces about 1.3 litres of urine a day. It holds a large share of the nitrogen and phosphorus we eat, but the flush sends it to the treatment plant, which recovers little of it. Separating urine at the source (urine-diverting toilets, dry urinals), storing it and then spreading it could replace part of the synthetic fertilisers made from fossil gas. The physiological nitrogen need is estimated at about 3.4 kg per person per year.

## 2. Where it stands

Source separation has been practised in Northern Europe and German-speaking countries since the 1990s. In France, the OCAPI research-action programme has studied the field since 2015. A Paris district, Saint-Vincent-de-Paul, is the first French urban project designed with source separation. Field trials have been running since 2024 on the Saclay plateau, in the Lyon area and in Angers, and an ADEME-funded project is testing urine concentration by freezing. The first trial results were not yet published at the end of 2024.

**Maturity: field-tested**, in pilot projects. There is no authorised widespread use.

## 3. Who is working on it

- The OCAPI programme, at the LEESU laboratory of the École des Ponts, a resource centre on the subject.
- Cerema, which supports the City of Paris.
- The French national federation of organic farming (FNAB), which decided to explore the subject in 2025.

## 4. Open questions

- Germs and medicine residues: these are the first concerns of the farmers surveyed.
- Nitrogen losses to the air during storage and spreading.
- Logistics: collecting, storing and transporting a bulky product.
- Regulation: urine-based fertilisers are not among the materials authorised in organic farming.

## 5. How to contribute

- Install a urine-diverting dry toilet at home or in a community space (*Set up a dry toilet* skill), and document its use.
- Follow or join a pilot project near you; the OCAPI programme lists French initiatives.
- Report to CivicNode what works and what doesn't.
