# Store three days of drinking water and keep it fresh

A water supply set up on an ordinary day saves you looking for water on the day the tap gives nothing. It takes little equipment, a bit of space, and a reminder twice a year.

## 1. Principle

Unopened commercially bottled water is the safest supply. Otherwise, fill clean food-grade containers yourself with tap water. Either way, store the supply somewhere cool, out of the sun, away from toxic products, and replace water you bottled yourself every six months. A supply that is never renewed is the most common mistake: the date written on each container is there to avoid it.

## 2. Safety

> **LEVEL: GREEN (circle)** — [Official label shown from Nodule Zero]
>
> Variant with containers disinfected with bleach: orange.
>
> Risks specific to this skill:
> - A container that has held a toxic product (bleach, pesticide, fuel) is never used to store drinking water, even washed.
> - Bleach variant: irritating to skin and eyes. Never mix it with another product, ventilate, keep children away, store the bottle out of their reach.
> - Weight: a full container is heavy. Prefer several small containers that anyone can carry, placed on the floor or on a low, sturdy shelf.
> - A supply stored near petrol, solvents or pesticides can absorb their fumes.

## 3. Step by step

### Step 1: Work out how much water you need

- **Action:** count at least 3.8 litres [TO CHECK: gallon conversion] per person per day, for at least three days, so a little over 11 litres per person [TO CHECK: three-day total]; two weeks if possible. Add extra for pregnant women, sick people, pets; in very hot weather, needs can double. Write down the total for the household.
- **Check:** a number of litres is written down for the whole household.
- **Source:** s1 (1 gallon per person per day, at least three days; two weeks if possible), cross-checked with s2 (1 gallon per person per day; needs that can double in hot weather). The official French quantity, which counts drinking water only, is given in “What the sources say”.

### Step 2: Choose the containers

- **Action:** preferably use commercially bottled water, left sealed. Otherwise, choose containers designed for food. Failing that, a container that closes tightly, unbreakable (no glass), with a narrow opening if possible. Rule out any container that has held a toxic product.
- **Check:** each container chosen does not leak when turned upside down full, and you know what it held before.
- **Source:** s1

### Step 3: Wash the containers

- **Action:** for containers you fill yourself, wash the inside and the cap with soap, then rinse thoroughly. Sealed commercial bottles go straight to step 6.
- **Check:** no more foam or smell of soap in the container.
- **Source:** s1

### Step 4: Disinfect the containers (orange variant)

- **Action:** pour into the container a mixture of one teaspoon (about 5 millilitres) of regular bleach, unscented, not “colour safe” and with no added cleaner, to about 1 litre of water [TO CHECK: teaspoon and quart conversion]. Close it and shake so the mixture touches the whole inside wall. Wait at least 30 seconds, empty it, then let it air-dry.
- **Check:** the whole inside wall has been wetted by the mixture, and the container is dry before filling.
- **Source:** s1 (1 teaspoon of bleach at 5–9% sodium hypochlorite per quart, or 4 cups, of water; 30 seconds) [TO CHECK: dose for bleach sold in France, often less concentrated than the product the source describes], cross-checked with s2 (same dose, with regular unscented bleach without additives)

### Step 5: Fill and close

- **Action:** fill the container with drinking water from the tap and close it with a tight cap.
- **Check:** the full container, laid on its side for a moment, does not leak.
- **Source:** s1

### Step 6: Label and date

- **Action:** write “drinking water” and the filling date on each container. On commercial bottles, find the date printed by the manufacturer.
- **Check:** each container shows a readable date.
- **Source:** s1

### Step 7: Store the supply

- **Action:** store the containers somewhere cool, between about 10 and 21 °C [TO CHECK: conversion of 50 to 70 °F], out of direct sunlight, away from petrol, pesticides and other toxic products.
- **Check:** the supply is in the shade all day and no toxic product is stored next to it.
- **Source:** s1 (50 to 70 °F)

### Step 8: Renew every six months

- **Action:** put a reminder in a calendar six months after the filling date. On that day, empty the containers you filled yourself (the water can be used for watering or cleaning), then repeat steps 3 to 6. For commercial bottles, follow the manufacturer's date.
- **Check:** no date written on a container you filled yourself is more than six months old.
- **Source:** s1, cross-checked with s2

### Step 9: Draw water without contaminating it

- **Action:** when you use the supply, pour the water or draw it with a clean utensil. Do not put your hands in the water or touch the inside of the container.
- **Check:** nothing but a clean utensil has gone into the container; it is closed again after use.
- **Source:** s1

## 4. If you get stuck

- **Step 2 — no food-grade container at hand.** Buy bottled water when you go shopping, a few bottles at a time, until you reach the total from step 1.
- **Step 4 — no unscented bleach, or the concentration cannot be read.** Do not guess the dose: build the supply with commercially bottled water, which needs neither washing nor disinfection.
- **Step 7 — no cool place available.** Spread the supply over several places (bottom of a cupboard, under a bed), always in the shade and away from toxic products.
- **Step 8 — the date has passed or is unreadable, and the water is needed now.** Treat it before drinking (*Make water safe by boiling it* or *Disinfect water with chlorine* skills), then rebuild the supply.
- **The stored water smells of fuel or chemicals.** Do not drink it, even treated, and throw the container away.

## 5. Resources

### What the sources say

The CDC recommends at least one gallon of water per person per day for three days, and two weeks if possible; it presents unopened commercially bottled water as the safest supply (source s1). Ready.gov gives the same quantity, for drinking and hygiene, and the same six-month period for water you bottle yourself (source s2).

The French Government gives, for the 72-hour emergency kit, “6 litres per person in bottles” (source s3, as a reference) [TO CHECK: figure from a reference source, to be checked on the page]. The difference comes from what each source counts: drinking water only for the French figure, water for drinking, cooking and a little hygiene for the CDC. This skill uses the CDC quantity, the more cautious one.
